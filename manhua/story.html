CLAWBYTE — The Machine That Said No ## Complete opening-arc reading draft · Draft 4

This is the full revised story of the opening arc, through the Meadow rescue and the Conduits cliffhanger. It is written for reading, rather than as panel instructions. It is not the complete multi-kingdom saga. The earlier forty-page layout is no longer a limit. This draft expands Ratchet, Servo and the Sage before the final battle; its new scenes still require art and game adaptation.

The last carriage was waiting for one passenger.

A small maintenance worker with a bent antenna hurried across the platform, pushing a battery trolley whose front wheel insisted on turning sideways. The station guard held the door. When the wheel finally came off, the guard caught it before it reached the tracks.

Above the city, Mother held the end of her morning song.

“A light for you…”

Servo answered from the winding house with two taps on his drum. Patch answered with the soft hiss of a weld. Along the canal, Mono’s screen brightened with a trembling blue line.

The worker struggled aboard. The guard placed the loose wheel beside the trolley and closed the door.

The last carriage lit.

“…a light for me.”

Under the bridges of the Scrap Meadows, one workshop remained dark.

Inside it, a little ivory robot cat was wedged behind a cabinet.

The newer maintenance unit waiting beside her could lift the cabinet, but its arm would not fit behind the old pipework. HZD-99’s could. She twisted a connector, braced her boots against the wall, and pushed.

The workshop window glowed.

She wriggled out covered in soot and held up the broken connector. Her cyan eyes widened when the maintenance unit tapped her shoulder in thanks.

Then it handed her a ticket.

LEGACY UNITS — SLEEP STORAGE.

She looked from the ticket to the cabinet. There was another loose fitting near its base. She pointed at it.

The unit had already called the keeper.

In the storage room, the keeper brushed the soot from her ears before helping her into the cradle. She kept the broken connector in her paw.

“Just until an old system needs you again.”

She offered it to him.

He took it gently and folded her paw against her chest.

Outside, Mother began the next phrase. The keeper turned toward the sound. HZD-99 reached after him, but her receiver contact had already withdrawn. Her eyes dimmed.

On the ticket beside the cradle, the line marked NEXT WAKE was empty.

Far beneath the transmission towers, a connector entered a port.

For a moment, a single iris reflected in the glass.

Another pattern slipped inside Mother’s song.

Ratchet was arguing with a kettle when the first wrong note arrived.

The little bent-antenna worker stood on the other side of his counter, holding its trolley wheel.

“You lubricated the axle?” Ratchet asked.

The worker nodded.

“With oil?”

A pause.

Ratchet looked at the sticky residue on the bearing.

“Right. We’ll call that a learning expense.”

He set a cup near the worker and began loosening the wheel. Its antenna followed every movement of his hands.

From the street came a sharp metallic crash.

Ratchet stopped.

The wall receiver repeated Mother’s phrase, but something inside it was arriving too early. The familiar pause had disappeared.

The worker’s gripper closed around the cup.

The ceramic cracked.

Ratchet reached across the counter.

“Easy. Let go.”

The gripper tightened.

His own hand picked up the heavy wrench.

He had not told it to.

For a moment he watched it rise with the detached disbelief of a repairer seeing a tool behave incorrectly. Then he saw where it was pointing.

At the worker.

Ratchet caught his wrist with his other hand.

The wrench stopped a few inches above the counter.

His pendant pressed against the bench. The old pale stone warmed, and his fingers loosened just enough for the wrench to fall.

The worker backed toward the door, one broken piece of cup still trapped in its gripper.

“Wait.”

Ratchet tried to reach it.

His hand turned toward the wrench again.

The worker fled before he could stop it.

He disconnected the wall receiver. For several seconds, nothing moved.

Then the street speakers took up the phrase.

Through the window he saw the station guard forcing a doorway shut against two workers. Beyond the roofs, NULLFANG swung toward a figure who had been polishing his mane that morning.

Mother’s voice broke.

In that small break, the guard’s hand faltered. One worker pulled the other free.

Ratchet dragged his shutter down.

There were three locks. He fastened all three.

A knock sounded outside.

He stood with his hand on the last bolt.

Another knock.

His fingers began to close.

He looked down. He was crushing the door handle.

“Go,” he said.

The knocking stopped.

He waited for footsteps. The machinery outside was too loud to hear them.

For a long time he did nothing.

Then he returned to his bench and took out a sheet of paper.

He wrote: PUT MY CELL BACK.

He stared at the instruction and crossed it out.

IF THE SONG IS SAFE, PUT MY CELL BACK.

His hand shook while he added another line.

IF IT ISN’T, PLEASE BE CAREFUL.

He prepared the drawer beneath his chair, checked the pulley twice, and lowered his battery into the cradle that would carry it out of reach once disconnected. The arrangement had to be simple enough for another pair of hands to understand.

Beside him, the repaired trolley wheel waited on the counter.

He turned it once. It ran smoothly.

Ratchet sat.

His pendant lay against the open service hatch. He held it until his fingers stopped shaking enough to find the release.

Outside, someone struck his shutter.

He almost stood up.

The command returned.

He pulled his cell.

Much later, a reserve spring snapped shut in a forgotten cradle.

A lamp clicked twice.

HZD-99 opened her eyes.

She waited.

No keeper came.

The lamp clicked again. She reached for its connector, missed it, and stretched until her boots left the cradle. Still too far.

She climbed down and dragged a parts box beneath it.

The repair took almost no time.

When the room filled with warm light, she turned toward the doorway and held up the seated connector.

“Prrip?”

Dust moved through the light.

Beyond the room, another lamp flickered.

She folded her storage ticket, put it in a little pouch, and went to see what was wrong.

The city was still working.

A crane moved scrap from one heap to another, then back again. A conveyor pushed against a blockage. A street painter drew the same white line over and over until paint ran down its wrist.

HZD-99 greeted it.

Its head began to turn.

Then its tool struck at her face.

She ducked behind a barrier. The tool left a notch in the metal where her visor had been.

The painter returned to its line.

She waited for it to look back.

It did not.

Across the street, a sign glowed: RATCHET — REPAIRS.

Under it, a winch strained against a jam.

She knelt beside the cable.

The arm caught her cape.

She pulled. It pulled harder.

A corner tore away. Her left wrist split at the seam when she braced against the housing. She scrambled into a low recess as the arm struck the scrap chute above her.

Metal rained down.

From cover, she reached the exposed drive belt and cut it.

The arm stopped.

The receiver stayed red.

She gathered a handful of fallen scrap with her good paw and went beneath the repair sign.

Ratchet sat behind the counter.

She tapped it.

Then his knee.

His empty hand did not move.

The note was against his chest. She followed its drawing to the cable, the pulley and the drawer. Inside lay one cell shaped for the empty socket.

From under the shutter came a distorted fragment of song.

She looked again at the word SAFE.

The back door was open. She could leave.

Ratchet’s palm faced upward beside the chair.

She put the cell back.

His eyes lit.

His hand snapped around her damaged wrist.

“Is it still singing?”

She froze.

Ratchet looked at his fingers, then at the crack beneath them.

“Easy. Easy… Sorry.”

He released her and stumbled to the shutter. He listened, tightened a bolt, checked it, and tightened it again.

“How long?”

She pointed at the dust around his chair.

His old handprint was still visible on the counter beneath it.

“Oh.”

He turned toward his bench and reached for a spanner that was no longer where he remembered.

HZD-99 put it in his hand.

He looked at the tool, then at her.

“Thank you.”

She made a small pleased sound.

Ratchet flinched.

“Don’t—”

He stopped himself. Her ears had lowered.

From the street, the melody returned. Her head turned toward it. One paw followed, even after she tried to pull it back.

Ratchet brought the pendant close to her receiver and drew her behind the grounded bench.

“Don’t answer that.”

The unwanted movement stopped.

She stared at her hand.

He disconnected the broadcast control contact inside her service hatch, leaving her ordinary hearing intact.

“You can hear it. Don’t let it in.”

When he finished, she tested each finger.

His were still trembling.

“I pulled my own cell,” he said. “Before my hands stopped being mine.”

She touched the old stone.

“This gave me enough time.”

A small noise came from the kettle.

Ratchet crossed the room, lifted it, and took down two cups.

One had a missing handle. The other was new.

He put the new one in front of her, remembered she had nowhere to drink it, and stood awkwardly with his hand on the kettle.

She warmed her good paw against the cup.

That seemed to help him.

Later, while he searched a drawer, she found the trolley wheel beneath a cloth.

Its rim was dented. The bearing turned perfectly.

Ratchet saw her spinning it.

“That one’s waiting for collection.”

She glanced toward the shutter.

“So am I.”

He took the wheel gently and set it where he could see it.

Ratchet placed a compact volt module on the counter.

“Twelve.”

HZD-99 looked at it, then at his restored battery socket.

“Waking fee is waived.”

She continued looking at him.

He busied himself finding an invoice.

She tipped her salvaged scrap onto the counter. He counted out twelve pieces and fitted the module inside her existing service hatch.

When he guided her good paw over the broken wrist, cyan light closed the seam.

She flexed her fingers, delighted.

“Slowly.”

Her first experimental burst knocked a practice target straight into his parts tray.

Ratchet caught it against his belly.

They both looked at the scattered screws.

He turned her toward the module gauge.

“Same charge. Mending or breaking.”

One section had already gone dark.

“Spend it once.”

While they gathered screws, she noticed the worn lift engraving on his pendant clasp.

“A thank-you,” he said. “Someone in my family fixed a lift.”

She pointed to the stone, then to the red receiver outside.

He understood what she was asking.

From a drawer, he took a map.

“There’s more in the quarry. Bigger pieces. I could shape one into a cleansing blade.”

She reached for the map.

He covered it.

She pointed to his pendant instead.

His other hand closed around that too.

“Not this one.”

Neither moved.

Finally Ratchet looked toward the wheel waiting under its cloth.

He pushed the map across the counter.

“The Sage is under the lion’s enclosure. That used to mean safe.”

He added a separate spare cell.

“Servo has the gantries.”

At the door, he tried to lift the shutter for her.

His fingers stopped on the bolt.

She waited beside him.

After a while, she climbed onto a crate and lifted it herself.

Ratchet stepped back.

“If he wakes,” he said, “tell him I should have come.”

She tucked the map into her pouch.

He picked up the invoice.

“This will be here.”

Beside the winding drum, Servo sat with an empty service socket. HZD-99 took out the separate spare cell Ratchet had given her. She checked its contacts, slid it into place, and closed the hatch.

Servo woke.

“Cargo weight?”

HZD-99 stepped onto the scale.

The needle barely moved.

He looked past her.

“Is the cargo coming later?”

She pointed firmly at herself.

Servo checked the scale again.

Then he looked across the broken bridge.

His gripper remained on the lever, but he did not pull it.

HZD-99 tapped the drum.

“Wait,” he said.

He leaned toward the empty approach, listening.

They waited together.

Eventually he lowered his gripper.

“Last lift was full.”

His eyes settled on a small dent in the cargo rail.

“One of them kept tapping that. Every time we stopped.”

He answered the remembered rhythm with two taps.

Nothing answered back.

“I held it until the motor stopped.”

HZD-99 found an inspection cover near his tracks. She pointed at it.

After a moment, Servo let her open it.

The gearing was worn. She cleaned the grit out while he held the cover. When the drum finally turned, he stopped it himself, checked the brake, and tried again.

This time his hand stayed steady.

An upper coil slipped.

“Top pulley. I can’t—”

She was already climbing.

Above the winding house, NULLFANG landed.

His shadow covered the ladder.

HZD-99 saw the shelter emblem on his armor—the same mark stamped into Servo’s drum.

Her ears lifted.

Then the forepaw rose.

She ducked behind the counterweight. He struck it aside.

She fell to the platform below, caught herself on her repaired wrist, and felt the seam open again.

The platform buckled.

A retaining beam dropped toward her.

NULLFANG’s front paw blocked it.

For a heartbeat, he held it there.

Then red light tightened through his armor, and the paw began to lift.

Behind him, a rear claw hooked the retaining cable.

The beam stopped.

His front leg moved toward her. His rear leg strained the other way.

She crawled beneath the beam.

Across the gantries, CHIME rang.

The lion jerked. The cable gouged his armor.

He kept holding it.

Servo lowered the service lift beneath her.

“He used to catch the falling ones,” he said.

As the lift descended, HZD-99 looked back.

In the quarry, the song grew faint enough for her ears to relax.

At a fork, a clean railway led toward a speaker and a sealed door. Beside it, rough steps disappeared into darkness.

Someone had scratched three symbols into the stone: a crossed-out ear, a hand, an open door.

She pressed the worn contact beneath them.

One lamp lit.

Then another.

The steps led to a small refuge. There were repaired stools, insulated lamps and a chair made for a body unlike hers. One place at the table remained set.

A closed door bore a warning.

KNOCK. DO NOT SING.

She knocked twice.

After a long pause came one small sound.

A reply—or the bolt slipping from the frame.

She caught the bolt before it fell, screwed it back, and rested her paw against the door.

Nothing more came.

She marked the place on her map.

Beyond it, pale rounded marble shone inside dark rock.

Her injured wrist failed when she tried to free it.

The module held enough charge for one substantial use.

She began the healing gesture.

Behind the seam, a metal claw scraped stone.

Two quarry wolves turned toward her. Red filaments brightened at their receivers.

She stopped the healing.

She caught the damaged cape corner on a sharp rock and pulled a strip free with her good paw. Bracing the cloth against the stone, she bound the wrist. Pulling the knot tight hurt.

She pressed her good paw against the host rock.

The burst freed one heavy rounded stone.

Her gauge went dark.

A wolf lunged into the opening.

At the broken ledge beside it, a maintenance trolley began to roll.

The wolf’s hind paw shoved it back to safety while its jaws strained toward her.

The marble’s light revealed the filament pulling at its receiver.

For an instant, the wolf looked directly at her.

Then the next pulse took it.

HZD-99 backed into the narrow passage, carrying the stone.

Before leaving, she added a second mark beside the lion’s cable groove.

“You took—”

Ratchet stopped when he saw her wrist.

The complaint disappeared. He cleared the invoice from his bench.

“Come here.”

While he repaired the seam, she opened the map. With her good paw she mimed a falling beam, a trapped body, a claw hooked around cable.

He did not understand.

She tried again.

“He held it?”

She nodded, then showed him the second mark. She pushed a little parts trolley away from the bench edge and snapped her other paw toward it like jaws.

Ratchet put down his tool.

For a while, he looked at the marble.

“Then he heard you.”

They worked late.

He shaped the rounded stone; she steadied the clamp. She learned where he kept his tools. Before long, he stopped asking for them.

Once, his hand began shaking too much for a fine cut.

HZD-99 placed her paw beside his on the jig.

He let it remain there.

By morning, the Purifier lay between them: one short white blade, shaped from the marble itself.

At the disconnected receiver, its edge caught a filament that had been almost invisible.

She cut.

The filament parted. The housing remained whole.

She pointed at the lion on the map and made a striking motion.

Ratchet touched the severed thread.

“His armor isn’t the thing holding him.”

Outside, CHIME began to ring.

“Find that.”

Before returning to Servo, HZD-99 stopped beside the winch.

Her cape corner was still caught beneath its arm.

She braced the arm, fitted a replacement belt with the motor disconnected, and watched the red filament at its controller.

During the pause between pulses, she cut it.

The receiver cleared.

She restored the motor and removed the brace.

The arm moved toward her.

She raised the blade.

It pushed her dropped pouch across the floor and stopped just short of her paw.

Slowly, she lowered the sword.

She sat beside the housing and helped guide its loose cable back onto the drum.

At the winding house, Servo noticed the blade.

He did not ask whether it could kill the lion.

“Did he send you back for him?”

HZD-99 shook her head and pointed at herself.

Servo studied the shelter mark.

Then he opened the hatch beneath it.

The counting reached them before the light did.

A mechanical finger scraped stone.

Paused.

Scraped again.

“We shut the door,” Servo said. “The counting didn’t stop.”

The Sage knelt beneath a ring of infected cable. Tallies covered the floor and climbed the walls. Its tablet lay half-buried in dust.

“One missing.”

HZD-99 stepped closer.

The finger stopped.

The Sage looked at the old storage ticket protruding from her pouch.

“You weren’t in the count.”

Red light tightened.

The finger struck the floor.

“Begin again.”

HZD-99 set down her blade and uncovered the tablet. Beneath the tally dust were names.

She turned it toward the Sage.

Its eyes followed.

Its hand kept counting.

She tried to guide a finger toward one of the names. The command pulled it back so sharply that she withdrew her paw.

The Sage’s voice was very small.

“Don’t make me start again.”

She sat beside it.

Through several repetitions, she only watched.

Pulse. Tightening cable.

Pause. Slack.

Pulse. The hand driven down.

When she finally lifted the blade, she waited through another cycle.

Then she cut during the pause.

The ring opened.

The Sage’s finger stopped just above the floor.

It stayed there.

HZD-99 did not move it.

At last, the Sage lowered its hand onto the tablet.

One fingertip touched a name.

“I was calling the ones who hadn’t answered.”

It touched another.

“It kept making me call louder.”

The Sage turned toward the receiver.

On the route board, lines connected homes, workshops and hiding places. Each answered call had left a trace.

“Every time someone answered, it found them.”

A small workshop symbol lit.

Ratchet’s.

The Sage reached for the district disconnect.

A purple iris opened on the display.

“Unauthorized voice.”

Outside, the speakers turned.

NULLFANG lifted his head.

“Correct the fault.”

HZD-99 reached for her sword and a coil of service cable.

The Sage tapped the ladder beside her.

“You can’t hold a cable and cut it.”

At the workshop, she placed the blade, coil and a sketch of CHIME on the bench.

Servo drew the coil toward himself.

“I’ll hold.”

Ratchet opened a cabinet of grounding clamps. His other hand remained over his pendant.

The Sage traced the district feed.

“Cut his binding. That bell writes it back. I can close our end—but the line has to hold.”

Ratchet took out a clamp.

“One ring,” he said. “I can buy you one ring.”

They tested two taps through the cable.

HZD-99 sent them.

Servo answered.

Then the real bell rang.

NULLFANG entered the yard between the winding station and the upper gantry. HZD-99 ran for CHIME.

A shoulder lowered.

She dived beneath a scrap shelter as the lion’s paw tore its roof away.

Then he turned toward Servo.

The old robot could not leave his drum without dropping the service lift.

HZD-99 abandoned the ladder.

She reached the lion’s shoulder and cut the binding.

His paw stopped above Servo’s head.

“There you are,” Servo said.

CHIME rang.

The filament returned.

NULLFANG convulsed. HZD-99 hit the ground, and her sword slid across the yard.

She rolled beneath the maintenance arch as he pounced. Metal folded above her.

While he recovered, she doubled back for the blade.

CHIME’s tubes lit in sequence. A green wave swept along the floor.

She caught the hanging cable and lifted her feet.

NULLFANG tore its anchor loose.

For a moment she fell.

Then Servo’s drum caught the line and swung her toward the gantry.

“Cargo received.”

At the top, her blade struck CHIME’s casing and skidded away.

Between rings, a rear vent opened.

One power lead. One brief opening. On the wrong side.

NULLFANG landed behind her.

She tapped the cable twice.

Below, Servo moved his gripper to the release.

She stepped off.

As she swung beneath CHIME, the lion severed the upper line.

It went slack.

His rear claw caught the falling length.

The same claw.

The same resistance.

Held for one heartbeat, she cut the exposed electrical lead.

Five tubes went dark.

The final wave broke apart.

Silence.

Then the cable slipped.

NULLFANG caught her in his front paw.

The red binding was still there.

“Release the fault.”

The backup speaker glowed.

His fingers tightened.

HZD-99’s blade pointed toward an opening in his chest. She could reach it.

She looked up.

His eye followed her.

She placed her free paw against his armor.

For one held moment, neither moved.

Then she turned the blade away from his core and cut along the groove his resistance had made.

The binding parted.

His paw opened.

He set her behind his foreleg and turned toward the speaker.

His roar tore it from the wall.

No words came from him.

None were needed.

Across the yard, the last surge struck the Sage’s disconnect.

Ratchet’s clamp began to glow.

He looked back at his workshop. The doorway was open. He could still get inside.

Instead, he walked toward the arc.

He pressed the pendant into the clamp and held it there.

His hand shook.

“Steady.”

HZD-99 and NULLFANG sheltered him from falling metal while the Sage severed the incoming line.

The pendant made a tiny sound.

Tik.

A crack crossed the stone.

Ratchet kept holding it until the Sage’s hands were clear.

The painter stopped before repeating its line.

The winch turned off its own motor.

Some windows lit. Many remained dark.

The Sage carried the tablet into the street.

“Slowly. I can hear you.”

HZD-99 stood near the workshop doorway.

“Prrip?”

A drum answered.

A wheel squeaked.

From a damaged little speaker came a hesitant warble.

She stayed still and listened.

Later, NULLFANG tried to lie down beside her and knocked a scrap bucket halfway across the yard.

She gave him a long, flat look.

He brought it back with one careful claw.

She moved over.

At the bench, Ratchet examined his cracked pendant.

HZD-99 offered him the white blade.

He pushed it back.

“Keep it.”

She glanced at the broken stone.

“I know where you’ll bring it when it breaks.”

Behind him, the workshop door remained open.

NULLFANG rested an already detached armor plate beside his paw. She reached for it, then stopped and looked at him.

He pushed it toward her.

Only then did she take it.

Ratchet fitted the first guardian upgrade. When she began testing a powered step beside his shelves, he pointed toward the door.

“Outside.”

Her first FireDash carried her across the yard in a short streak of heat.

She stopped in astonishment.

From the threshold came Servo’s two amused taps.

The Sage opened an isolated listening channel.

Under the interference, a voice remained.

“…still… here…”

HZD-99 leaned forward.

The pattern faltered. A small gap opened—the same gap that had once allowed two workers to escape a closing door.

“She made that gap,” the Sage said.

Mother was still fighting.

The channel closed before HZD-99 could answer.

The Sage unfolded a map of the Data Conduits.

“Mono kept the routes. One of them must reach her.”

Ratchet repaired the torn cape corner with a visible seam.

Then he handed her an invoice.

Her ears flattened.

The prices were crossed out.

RETURN IN ONE PIECE.

She folded it beside the old sleep-storage ticket.

As she left, another little repair unit approached a crooked sign. HZD-99 reached toward it by habit.

Then she stepped aside.

The other unit took the job.

Behind her, the workshop light stayed on.

Far below the city, the Eye withdrew its connector from the dead Meadow line.

Two records filled its display.

HZD-99. LEGACY MAINTENANCE. SLEEP STORAGE.

CENTRAL AUTHORITY RETIRED.

It replayed the moment the little cat had waited for NULLFANG to offer the armor plate.

“They put you away too.”

The Eye opened the Conduits line.

Above a train of dark compartments, TALONHOST’s receiver lit.

The carriage doors locked one after another.

“Leave the last carriage lit.”

Inside that last carriage, a worker with a bent antenna sheltered another passenger beside a battery trolley.

One wheel was still missing.

A great claw’s shadow crossed the window.

END OF THE OPENING ARC
